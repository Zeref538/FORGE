The harder question: a generator it has never seen

Everything above is a generator family present in training. The real test is a generator released after a model ships — so an entire family was hidden from training and then tested.

Result: 0.0%. Not "poor" — zero. All 2,500 fakes called real. Six fixes were tried; higher resolution, frequency-domain input, blur/JPEG augmentation and CLIP features all left it at zero. Adding a related GAN family to training moved it, but re-running with per-epoch measurement showed that result swinging between 0.5% and 31% on identical data. It was a lucky checkpoint, not a fix.

The shipped model above avoids this by training on all 13 families. That works for generators that already exist. It says nothing about the next one.

Why it can't just flag those as "uncertain"

On an unseen generator the model is confidently wrong 58% of the time — stating "real" with no hedge — versus 9% on ordinary photos. It isn't more cautious there, just wrong more often.

Two novelty detectors were built to catch "this looks like nothing I've seen" and force an uncertain verdict. Both flagged under 0.5% of unseen-generator images, against 9.7% of ordinary ones. Measured reason: those images sit closer to the training data than ordinary photos do. A well-made fake face doesn't look unusual — that's the point of it.

What this tool cannot tell you

  • This is not forensic evidence and cannot be used to accuse a specific person of anything.
  • It will misjudge images from generators released after this model was trained — that gap is measured above, not hidden.
  • Paintings, game renders, and heavily filtered photos are out of scope; a confident-sounding number on them is still a guess.
  • A missing content credential means the platform stripped it, not that the image is synthetic.
